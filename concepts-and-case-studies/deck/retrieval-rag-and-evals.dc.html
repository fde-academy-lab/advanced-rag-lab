Mental model · added for the advanced track

The pipeline is a recall budget spent on precision

STAGE 1 · CHEAP
First-stage retrieval
Buys recall. Optimise Recall@N with N in the hundreds. Anything lost here is lost permanently.
knob: N, hybrid weights, chunking
STAGE 2 · EXPENSIVE
Reranking
Converts recall into precision. Cannot exceed the ceiling set by stage one.
knob: model class, candidate depth
STAGE 3 · SCARCE
Context packing
A fixed token budget. Every distractor admitted costs a slot a gold chunk could have used.
knob: k, dedup, ordering
STAGE 4 · JUDGED
Generation
Turns evidence into a claim. Grounding failures here are independent of retrieval quality.
knob: instructions, abstention, citations
The single most common architectural mistake: tuning stage two or three to fix a recall problem created in stage one. Measure Recall@N before you touch the reranker.

Failure points: retrieval and evaluation

Lexical gap
“retry delay” does not match a document that only says “backoff interval.”
Missing hop
One of two required documents is absent from top-k; the answer uses only half the evidence chain.
Distractor dominance
A high-scoring but irrelevant passage crowds out the required source.
Metric blind spot
Answer accuracy looks stable while Evidence Recall@10 has regressed.
Case study · retrieval quality
Anthropic · Contextual Retrieval, 2024

Chunks lose the context that made them findable

A chunk reading “revenue grew 3% over the previous quarter” is unretrievable for the query “ACME Q2 2023 revenue growth” — the chunk never names the company or the quarter.
The reported fix runs at index time: prepend a short model-generated description that situates each chunk inside its parent document, then embed and BM25-index the augmented chunk.
Reported effect on failed retrievals
−49%
contextual embeddings + contextual BM25
−67%
the same, with a reranking stage added
The engineering read
Index-time compute is usually cheaper than query-time compute. You pay once per chunk; you pay per query forever.
Prompt caching changed the economics. Re-reading the whole document for every chunk is only affordable because the document sits in a cached prefix.
They kept BM25. A widely-cited “dense is enough” assumption did not survive contact with identifiers and exact terms.

YES

HLD · added for the advanced track

Keeping an index fresh without a nightly full rebuild

INCREMENTAL PATH · MINUTES
1 · Change capture. Source webhook or CDC stream emits document IDs, not documents.
↓
2 · Content hash diff. Re-chunk only documents whose normalised body hash changed. Metadata-only edits skip embedding entirely.
↓
3 · Chunk-level upsert. Stable chunk IDs derived from doc_id + ordinal + hash. Delete-then-insert orphans; upsert-then-tombstone does not.
↓
4 · Soft-delete sweep. Tombstoned chunks stay filterable until the next compaction so in-flight queries stay consistent.
REBUILD PATH · HOURS TO DAYS
Triggered by an embedding-model change, a chunking change, or an analyzer change — never by content.
↓
Build index v(n+1) alongside v(n). Both are queryable; only one is routed to.
↓
Shadow-evaluate v(n+1) on the frozen eval slice and on replayed production queries before any traffic moves.
↓
Atomic alias swap, keep v(n) warm. Rollback is a pointer change, not a rebuild.
Mixing the two paths is the classic outage: never write new-model embeddings into an index that still holds old-model vectors. Vectors from different models are not comparable, and cosine similarity will not tell you they are wrong.

Enterprise reality · added for the advanced track

Permission-aware retrieval and multi-tenancy

POST-FILTER · WRONG
Retrieve top-k globally, then drop what the user may not see.
Two failures, both silent: k collapses — a user with narrow access gets two chunks instead of eight; and scores leak — the existence and ranking of restricted documents is observable through result counts and latency.
PRE-FILTER · RIGHT
Push the ACL predicate into the ANN search itself, so the candidate pool is already scoped.
Costs recall on graph indexes when the filter is very selective — HNSW traversal can dead-end. Mitigate with per-tenant namespaces or a partitioned index.
DESIGN CHECKLIST
Where does the ACL live at query time? Denormalised onto the chunk, or resolved per request from the source system?
How fast must a revocation propagate? Minutes means denormalised ACLs need their own CDC stream.
Namespace or filter? Namespaces isolate cleanly and cost you cross-tenant recall; filters share an index and cost you tuning.
Is the cache tenant-scoped? A shared prompt-prefix cache across tenants is a data-leak class of bug.
Do traces store retrieved text? If yes, the trace store inherits the corpus's compliance boundary.

Case study · retrieval representation
LinkedIn · knowledge-graph RAG for customer support, 2024

Flat chunking destroyed the structure the answer depended on

THE PROBLEM
Historical support tickets are structured objects: issue, environment, steps tried, resolution, linked tickets.
Split into flat text chunks, a retrieved fragment could return the symptom while leaving the resolution in a different chunk that never scored high enough.
WHAT THEY BUILT
Tickets parsed into a knowledge graph whose nodes preserve ticket sections and whose edges preserve relationships between tickets.
Retrieval runs over sub-graphs, so the unit returned to the model is a coherent case, not a fragment.
REPORTED OUTCOME
−28.6%
median per-issue resolution time, alongside gains in retrieval and answer quality metrics.
The engineering read. Before tuning a retriever, ask what the natural retrievable unit of this corpus is. For tickets it is a case. For contracts it is a clause. For code it is a symbol and its call sites.
The cost. A parser per source system, a graph store to operate, and a schema that has to be maintained as the ticket template changes. This is worth it when the corpus is highly structured and high volume — and rarely otherwise.

Retrieval methods, embedding models, and reranking

Lexical retrieval: token-level matching over an inverted index.
Semantic retrieval: dense-vector similarity over an ANN index.
Grep: literal or regular-expression search over local artifacts.
Reranking: a more expressive scorer applied to a small candidate set.

Retrieval is usually staged: use a fast first-stage method to produce candidates, then spend more compute only where it improves ranking quality.

Section 4 · Retrieval & reranking

Lexical retrieval: when it is the right tool

It is strong for identifiers, error codes, API names, dates, and exact terminology.
It is deterministic, explainable, inexpensive, and easy to filter by metadata.
It benefits from domain vocabulary that may be poorly represented by an embedding model.
Its main limitation is the lexical gap: it cannot match synonyms or paraphrases unless they share terms.
Section 4 · Retrieval & reranking

Lexical retrieval: inverted index and BM25

Documents
→
Analyze and tokenize
→
Inverted index term → postings
←
Query tokens
→
BM25 scoring
→
Top-k documents
SCORE(q, d)
Σi  IDF(qi)  ·  f(qi,d) · (k₁+1)f(qi,d) + k₁ · (1 − b + b · |d|/avgdl)
IDF — rare terms carry more weight than common ones.
k₁ ≈ 1.2–2.0 — term-frequency saturation. The tenth occurrence adds far less than the second.
b ≈ 0.75 — length normalisation. b=0 ignores document length; b=1 fully normalises it.
Chunking changes avgdl. Re-chunking silently re-tunes your BM25 scores — re-measure after every chunking change.

BM25 combines inverse document frequency, term-frequency saturation, and document-length normalization to score a query–document pair.

Semantic retrieval: when it adds signal

Dense retrieval matches semantic similarity, so it can recover paraphrases and related concepts.
It is useful when the query wording differs from the corpus wording.
It can still miss rare identifiers, negation, or precise constraints; hybrid retrieval often addresses this.
The embedding model, instruction prefix, chunking strategy, and domain alignment all affect recall.

Section 4 · Retrieval & reranking

Cosine similarity for dense retrieval

cos(q, d) = q · d‖q‖₂ ‖d‖₂
after L2 normalization  →  cos(q, d) = q · d
It measures the angle between the query vector and the document vector.
After L2 normalization, cosine similarity is equivalent to the dot product.
Use it for ranking; treat the score as relative, not as a calibrated probability.
Consequence for engineering: a fixed similarity cut-off is not portable across corpora, models, or even reindexes. If you need a threshold, calibrate it per deployment against labelled examples.

Hybrid retrieval: how the two lists get merged

RECIPROCAL RANK FUSION · rank-based
RRF(d) = Σr  1 / (k + rankr(d))   k ≈ 60
Robust. No score normalisation, no per-corpus tuning, no scale mismatch.
Stable across drift. A retriever whose score distribution shifts does not break the merge.
Throws away magnitude. A runaway top hit and a marginal one at the same rank contribute identically.
k is a real knob. Small k rewards rank-1 heavily; large k flattens toward a union of both lists.
WEIGHTED SCORE FUSION · score-based
s(d) = α · ŝdense(d)  +  (1−α) · ŝbm25(d)
Keeps confidence. A dominant exact-identifier match can still win outright.
Tunable per query class. α can be routed: higher for paraphrase queries, lower for code and IDs.
Needs normalisation. BM25 is unbounded, cosine is [−1,1]. Min-max over the candidate set is query-dependent and unstable.
α overfits. Tuned on 200 examples, it will not survive a corpus refresh.
Default to RRF. Move to weighted fusion only when you have a labelled set large enough to tune α per query class and a plan to re-tune it on a schedule.

Selecting an embedding model

Can the corpus leave the client's network?
NO
Self-hosted open-weight encoder. Budget GPU capacity for the initial backfill, not just steady state.
YES ↓
Is the domain vocabulary far from general web text — clinical, legal, telecom, internal jargon?
YES
Benchmark 3 candidates on your eval set. Public leaderboard order rarely survives a domain shift. Lean harder on the lexical leg.
NO ↓
Is the corpus multilingual, or are queries in a different language from the documents?
YES

NO ↓

YES

Section 4 · Retrieval & reranking

Grep: exact retrieval for agents

rg -n -i "retry|backoff" src tests
rg performs literal or regular-expression matching over files, with paths and line locations.
Agents use it to resolve symbols, locate call sites, inspect configuration, and trace implementation details.
It is precise and inspectable, but it does not bridge paraphrases or rank evidence semantically.
Grep complements vector retrieval when the task is anchored in a local repository or operational artifact.
Case study · architecture divergence
Code assistants · indexed retrieval vs. agentic grep

The same task, two production retrieval architectures

A · PRE-INDEXED SEMANTIC RETRIEVAL
Embed the repository, query at request time
Chunk by symbol, embed, keep the index synced to the working tree. A query returns semantically related code the developer could not have named.
Wins on “where is rate limiting handled?” — conceptual queries with no shared vocabulary.
Pays for index freshness on every branch switch, a remote embedding step that touches customer source, and stale results after a refactor.
B · AGENTIC GREP, NO INDEX
Let the model search the tree, turn by turn
The agent issues glob and regex searches, reads what it finds, and refines. Retrieval state is the transcript; nothing is precomputed.
Wins on exactness and freshness — it reads the file as it is on disk right now, and every step is inspectable.
Pays for multiple model turns per question: higher latency, higher token cost, and a hard dependency on the agent's search strategy.
The engineering read. Neither is wrong. Indexed retrieval buys latency and conceptual recall at the price of a synchronisation problem; agentic grep buys correctness and zero index operations at the price of tokens and turns. Choose by asking which failure the client cannot tolerate — a stale answer, or a slow one.
Section 4 · Retrieval & reranking

Early-interaction rerankers

Query
Candidate passage
↓
[CLS] q [SEP] passage [SEP]
↓
Cross-encoder
↓
Relevance score

Query and passage tokens attend to one another inside the encoder. Cross-encoders are high quality but require a forward pass for every query–candidate pair, so apply them only to the top N candidates.

Cost is linear in N. Reranking 100 candidates costs 100 forward passes — per query, every query.
Nothing can be precomputed. The representation depends on the pair, so there is no index to build.
Batch, do not loop. The difference between a batched and a serial reranker is often the difference between 90 ms and 900 ms.
Section 4 · Retrieval & reranking

Late-interaction rerankers

MaxSim(q, d)  =  Σi  maxj  qi⊤dj
ColBERT-style models encode query and passage tokens independently and retain token-level vectors.
Interaction happens at scoring time through maximum similarity for each query token.
Passage representations can be indexed offline, trading storage and index complexity for lower online latency than a cross-encoder.
Use late interaction when token-level precision is valuable but full cross-encoder scoring is too expensive.

Liu et al., “Lost in the Middle”, 2023

Position inside the context changes whether evidence is used

Holding the evidence set constant and moving only the position of the relevant document, accuracy traces a U: strongest when the gold document sits at the start or the end of the context, weakest in the middle.
gold doc firstmiddle of contextgold doc last
Illustrative shape of the reported effect, not measured values. Magnitude varies by model and task.
WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT
Keep k small. The cheapest mitigation is having fewer chunks in the middle at all.
Order by reranker score, then interleave. Place the two highest-scoring chunks at the head and the tail of the evidence block.
Restate the question after the evidence. A short recap at the tail puts the task in the strong position.
Test it, do not assume it. Run your own eval set with the gold chunk forced into position 1, mid, and last. The gap is your position sensitivity.

Method · added for the advanced track

Calibrating an LLM judge so you can trust its verdict

THE LOOP
1 · Write the rubric as a decision procedure, not an adjective. “Every factual claim maps to a span in a cited source” beats “is the answer faithful”.
2 · Two humans label 100–200 examples against the same rubric. Where they disagree, the rubric is ambiguous — fix the rubric, not the labels.
3 · Score judge-vs-human agreement with Cohen's κ, not raw accuracy. On a skewed set, a judge that always says “pass” scores 90% and is worthless.
4 · Iterate the rubric until κ clears your bar — and until judge–human agreement is at least as high as human–human agreement.
5 · Pin and version it. Judge model, temperature, rubric text, and few-shot examples are all release artifacts. Re-run the calibration set on every change.
KNOWN BIASES TO CONTROL FOR
Position bias. In pairwise comparison, swap A and B and require the verdict to hold.
Verbosity bias. Longer answers score higher. Cap length or normalise for it.
Self-preference. A judge tends to favour output from its own model family. Use a different family from the generator where you can.
Scale compression. A 1–10 scale collapses to 7s and 8s. Prefer binary or three-point rubrics with explicit criteria.
Leniency on grounding. Judges accept plausible unsupported claims. Force span-level citation checking rather than a holistic judgment.
Rule of thumb: use the judge for relative comparisons between two versions of your system. Treat its absolute score as a dashboard number, not a client-facing quality claim.

Case study · evaluation in production
DoorDash · RAG delivery-support chatbot, publicly reported

Two quality layers with two different latency budgets

INLINE · MILLISECONDS
Guardrail before the reply is sent
Checks the drafted response against the retrieved knowledge and against policy — grounding, compliance, and tone — and blocks or regenerates rather than shipping a bad answer.
Must be cheap: it sits inside the user-facing latency budget on every single turn.
OFFLINE · HOURS
LLM judge over sampled transcripts
Scores conversations on retrieval correctness, response accuracy, coherence, and policy adherence, then routes the failures back into prompt, retrieval, and knowledge-base fixes.
Can afford to be expensive: it runs on a sample, off the request path.

Equally important: the judge produced a labelled failure stream, which is what let the knowledge base itself be fixed rather than just the prompt.
The engineering read. Separate the thing that blocks from the thing that learns. A single evaluator asked to do both ends up too slow to block and too shallow to teach.
Ask in discovery. What is the cost of a wrong answer reaching this user? If it is regulatory, you need the inline layer on day one — not after the pilot.

HLD · added for the advanced track

The agentic search loop

1 · Decompose. Break the question into sub-questions with an explicit dependency order. “Who acquired B, and what was A's revenue that year” is two hops, not one query.
↓
2 · Select a tool. Lexical for identifiers, dense for concepts, grep for repositories, SQL for aggregates, a live API for anything the index cannot hold.
↓
3 · Retrieve and read. Append results to working evidence with their provenance. Never overwrite; the trace is the audit record.
↓
4 · Sufficiency check. Does the working evidence answer every sub-question? This is a separate, cheap model call with a strict schema — not a vibe inside the main prompt.
↰ NO · loop with a refined sub-questionYES ↓
5 · Synthesise and cite. Answer only from working evidence, with source IDs and an explicit abstention path.
WHAT GOES WRONG
Query drift. By turn four the agent is searching for something adjacent to the original question. Re-anchor on the original text every turn.
Evidence bloat. Working evidence grows past the context budget and the earliest, often best, results get dropped.
Tool thrash. The same query re-issued to three tools because none returned a confident result. Deduplicate issued queries.
Premature confidence. The sufficiency check passes on partial evidence, so a two-hop question gets a one-hop answer with full confidence.
Cost blowout. Nothing bounds the loop, and a single hard question costs forty times a normal one.

A STRONG ANSWER COVERS
Days 1–2: sample 100 real failures and hand-label each one against the fault-isolation tree — not retrieved, retrieved-not-used, or wrong despite good evidence.
Day 3: the distribution decides the plan. 70% retrieval misses is a chunking and hybrid problem; 70% grounding failures is a prompt and abstention problem. These have completely different four-week plans.
Days 4–5: stand up a regression set from those 100 cases plus null questions, and freeze a slice.
Reframe the target: some fraction of the 20% is unanswerable from the corpus. Split the goal into “answer correctly” and “refuse correctly” — refusing well is often the faster path to a client-acceptable system.
Red flags: jumping straight to “I'd try a better embedding model”; proposing three changes at once; accepting 95% as a well-defined target without asking how it is measured.
Interview bank · Q2
release judgment · stakeholder handling

“A retrieval change raises average answer quality 6%, but one business unit reports the system got worse. Do you ship it?”

WHAT THE INTERVIEWER IS TESTING
Whether you treat an aggregate as evidence or as a summary that hides its own counterexample.
Whether you can separate “is the complaint real” from “is the complaint decisive”.
Whether you will actually make a decision, or hide behind “it depends”.
A STRONG ANSWER COVERS
Reproduce before believing. Pull that unit's real queries, run both configs, and check whether the regression appears in the metrics. Perceived regressions are sometimes a UI or expectation change.
Find the mechanism. A unit-specific regression usually has a concrete cause: their corpus is identifier-heavy and you shifted fusion weight toward dense; or their documents are short and your chunking change altered avgdl.
Prefer a per-segment configuration over an all-or-nothing ship, if the mechanism supports it. Routing by query class is cheaper than losing a stakeholder.
Make the decision explicit and owned. If you ship despite the regression, that unit's owner hears it from you first, with the number and the remediation date.
Red flags: “the average improved, so we ship”; treating the complaint as politics; promising to fix it later with no mechanism and no date.
Interview bank · Q3
systematic debugging

“You upgraded the embedding model and Evidence Recall@10 fell from 0.86 to 0.71. Walk me through the diagnosis.”

WHAT THE INTERVIEWER IS TESTING
Whether you check operational causes before model-quality causes. A 15-point drop is almost never “the new model is worse”.
Whether you know the specific ways an encoder swap breaks a pipeline.
Whether you can bisect rather than guess.
THE CHECKLIST A STRONG CANDIDATE RUNS, IN ORDER
1 · Mixed-version index. Are some vectors still from the old model? Check the model-version tag on a sample.
2 · Prefix asymmetry. Does the new model require query and passage instruction prefixes? Are both applied on the right side?
3 · Normalisation and metric. Are the new vectors L2-normalised, and is the index still configured for cosine rather than raw dot product or L2 distance?
4 · Dimension truncation. Was the model shortened to fit the existing schema without checking the recall cost?
5 · Context-length truncation. Does the new encoder have a shorter input limit, silently cutting long chunks?
6 · ANN parameters. Was the graph rebuilt with the same efConstruction/M? Compare against flat search on a sample to isolate index loss from embedding loss.
7 · Only now: the model really is worse on this domain. Slice the misses by question type to show where.
Red flags: starting at step 7; not knowing that flat search gives you a ground-truth comparison; no plan to bisect.
Interview bank · Q4
enterprise constraints · security thinking

“Legal requires that no answer can be influenced by a document the user is not allowed to read. Design for that.”

WHAT THE INTERVIEWER IS TESTING
Whether you hear the word “influenced” and realise post-filtering does not satisfy it.
Whether you think about caches, logs, and traces as part of the security boundary — not just the index.
Whether you can name what this costs in recall and say so up front, rather than discovering it in UAT.
A STRONG ANSWER COVERS
Pre-filter, always. The ACL predicate goes into the ANN query so restricted chunks are never candidates — and never contribute to a score, a rank, or a rerank batch.
Name the recall cost. Selective filters degrade graph traversal. Mitigate with per-tenant namespaces or partitioned indexes, and measure recall with the filters on.
Close the side channels. Prompt caches keyed per tenant; traces stored inside the same compliance boundary as the corpus; result counts and latency not leaking the existence of restricted documents.
Revocation has an SLA. If ACLs are denormalised onto chunks, they need their own change-capture stream, and you should be able to state the propagation lag in minutes.
Prove it. An automated test that runs the same query as two personas and asserts disjoint evidence sets — part of the release gate, not a one-off.
Red flags: “filter the results after retrieval”; forgetting the cache; no answer for how a revoked permission propagates.
Interview bank · Q5
unit economics · saying no well

“Agentic search costs $0.90 on hard questions. Finance wants $0.15. What do you change, and what do you refuse to change?”

WHAT THE INTERVIEWER IS TESTING
Whether you can decompose a per-query cost into line items from memory.
Whether you optimise the distribution rather than the worst case — most traffic is not hard.
Whether you will push back with a quantified consequence instead of silently degrading quality.
A STRONG ANSWER COVERS
Reframe from worst case to blended cost. If 8% of queries are hard, the blended number may already be near target. Get the distribution before redesigning anything.
Escalate, do not loop by default. Single-shot first; enter the loop only when the sufficiency check fails. This alone usually removes most of the multiplier.
Cheap wins in order: cache the stable prefix and the tool schemas; carry a compacted evidence summary between turns instead of the full text; cap turns; use a small model for decomposition and sufficiency, and the large one only for synthesis.
What I refuse: removing the grounding and abstention checks, and removing the trace. Both are cheap and both are what stop a wrong answer from becoming an incident.
Quantify the residual. “I can reach $0.22 blended without quality loss. Getting to $0.15 means capping at two turns, which costs roughly X points of full-chain recall on multi-hop. That is a business decision — here is the number.”
Red flags: agreeing to the number without a plan; “we'll use a cheaper model” as the whole answer; no quality cost attached to any lever.
Interview bank · Q6
epistemics of measurement

“Your LLM judge says quality went up. How would you know if the judge is wrong?”

WHAT THE INTERVIEWER IS TESTING
Whether you treat the evaluator as a component that can regress, exactly like the retriever.
Whether you know agreement statistics, not just accuracy.
Whether you can name specific judge biases and the control for each.
A STRONG ANSWER COVERS
A held-out human-labelled calibration set that the judge is re-scored against on every judge, rubric, or model change. Track Cohen's κ over time as its own metric.
Compare judge–human agreement to human–human agreement. If two humans only agree 70% of the time, a judge at 72% is doing fine and the rubric is the problem.
Adversarial probes. Feed known-bad answers that are long, fluent, and confidently wrong. A judge that passes them has verbosity bias, not quality signal.
Cross-check with an independent signal. If judged quality rises while citation click-through, escalation rate, or thumbs-down do not move, believe the production signal.
Version everything. Judge model, temperature, rubric text, few-shot examples. An unexplained score jump with no system change is judge drift until proven otherwise.
Red flags: “we'd spot-check some outputs”; using raw agreement on a skewed set; using the same model family as generator and judge without noting self-preference.
